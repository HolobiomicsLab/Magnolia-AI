Useful, but narrowly — and I think the common framing of it is backwards.

The weak case is debate-to-consensus. When you let models argue until they agree, what you're mostly measuring is persuasiveness, not correctness. The more fluent or more confident model tends to pull the others, and models are quite willing to abandon a correct position under pressure. Much of the reported gain from multi-agent debate in the literature also survives an unflattering ablation: sample the same model N times and take a majority vote, and you recover a good fraction of it. A lot of what looks like "debate" is ensembling plus extra inference compute wearing a costume.

The strong case is cross-vendor disagreement as a detector. Different pretraining corpora and different post-training produce partially decorrelated failure modes — Gemini, Claude and DeepSeek generally don't hallucinate the same citation, the same reagent, or the same nonexistent keyword in a program's input file. So disagreement is a cheap, high-recall flag for "this claim needs a human or a verifier." That's a real signal you cannot get from self-consistency within one model, because a single model's errors are correlated with themselves. Use the ensemble to decide where to look, not what is true.

In your domain that distinction is unusually cheap to exploit, because you have external verifiers. A disagreement about a docking protocol, a basis set, or a restraint definition doesn't need to be settled rhetorically — each position can emit a runnable configuration and you settle it with HADDOCK3 or the actual calculation. Debate as a proposal diversifier feeding a verifier is defensible; debate as an arbiter is not.

Two costs specific to your situation, though.

It makes credit assignment worse, and that's already your unsolved core problem. Right now you can't attribute negative feedback to specific injected memory entries. Add three vendors arguing over several rounds and the attribution surface grows combinatorially — you'd need to log not just retrieved entry IDs per session but which agent consumed which entry at which round, and whose position survived into the final answer. If Mimosa's contribution is the inter-node coordination layer, multi-vendor debate is arguably the hardest possible test case for it. That could be a feature for the paper, but it's the wrong thing to bolt on before the logging substrate exists.

And it's bad for reproducibility, which matters for anything you publish. Vendor endpoints are moving targets — snapshots get deprecated, behavior shifts silently. A method whose results depend on a specific closed model's rhetorical style in 2026 is hard to reproduce in 2028. This actually argues for your open-weight direction: a debate ensemble of pinned open-weight checkpoints on Jean Zay is a scientifically reproducible artifact in a way that a cross-vendor API ensemble is not, and it sidesteps the export-control exposure too. You lose some decorrelation, since open-weight models share a lot of distillation ancestry, but you gain a method someone else can actually run.

So: worth building as a disagreement detector wired to a real verifier, on pinned weights, with per-agent provenance logged from day one. Not worth building as a general-purpose "let the models argue and take the winner" layer.
