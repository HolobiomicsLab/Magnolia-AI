---

### The two-domain machine

Hsc70 is a protein with two halves connected by a flexible tether:

```
  ┌─────────────────────┐         ┌──────────────────────┐
  │       NBD           │ ~~~~~~  │        SBD           │
  │   "fuel engine"     │ linker  │   "gripping hand"    │
  │   resi 1–385        │         │   resi 386–646       │
  └─────────────────────┘         └──────────────────────┘
```

- **NBD** (1–385): the engine. Burns ATP as fuel. When fueled, the machine is "open." When fuel is spent, it closes tight.
- **SBD** (386–646): the hand. Grabs other proteins marked for recycling.

---

### The gripping hand: palm + fingers

The SBD has two parts:

```
  ┌────────────────────────────┐
  │         SBDα (lid)         │  ← "fingers" — resi 531–615
  │     the "tri-helix"        │     a bundle of 5 α-helices
  │     helical bundle         │     folds over the palm
  ├────────────────────────────┤
  │         SBDβ (palm)        │  ← "palm" — resi 386–510
  │     rigid β-sandwich       │     has a groove for peptides
  │     with peptide groove    │
  └────────────────────────────┘
```

- **SBDβ** (the palm): a rigid platform with a slot cut into it. Model peptides sit here. **PDB 4PO2** is a crystal structure of this.
- **SBDα** (the fingers): the "tri-helix" — a bundle of 5 α-helices (A through E) that fold over the palm. When the machine closes, the fingers clamp down.

---

### The story changes: from one site to two

The scientific story evolved. Here's the timeline:

**Old view (pre-2025):**
Peptide sits in the palm groove. Fingers just close on top to lock it in. The fingers don't bind anything — they're just a lid.

**Sahu & Malhotra v1 (Jan 2025):**
"Wait — KFERQ peptides don't fit in the palm groove. They bind to the **fingers** themselves!" They found a pocket on the fingers (SBDα), formed between helix B and helix E. Since the fingers are at residues 531–615, this made the fingers THE binding site.

**Sahu & Malhotra v2 (Dec 2025):**
"Actually, both are right." They extended their study to full-length Hsc70 and found the answer is nuanced. There are **two** binding sites:

---

### The two pockets

#### Pocket 1 — Primary: palm groove (SBDβ)

When they ran AlphaFold3 on the **whole** Hsc70 protein (not just fingers), the KFERQ peptide went to the **palm groove** — for all 15 substrates. But with a critical catch:

- The palm groove only holds the peptide stably when the **fingers are present and folded down** (closed state)
- If you remove the fingers, the peptide falls out of the palm groove
- If the protein is in the "open" state (fingers lifted), the peptide is unstable

So the palm groove is the **primary** binding site, but the fingers are essential — without them capping it, the groove can't hold the peptide.

#### Pocket 2 — Secondary: finger cavity (SBDα)

The fingers themselves have a hidden pocket between two helices (B and E). This pocket IS real (confirmed by 44 μs of MD with different force fields), but binding there is **promiscuous** — different peptides hunker down in different ways. There's no single "correct" binding mode. The paper says this site "does not by itself account for substrate selectivity."

Two residues are standout hotspots: **Ile607** and **Tyr611** — they make contact in 8+ out of 15 substrates.

---

### What P2Rank saw (and didn't see)

When we fed full-length Hsc70 to P2Rank, it ranked pockets by how "druggable" they look:

| Rank | Score | What it is |
|------|-------|------------|
| 1 | 27.4 | NBD fuel pocket — deep and obvious |
| 2 | 8.7 | Mixed surface patch |
| 3 | 3.2 | **SBDβ palm groove** — Pocket 1 above |
| — | nothing | **SBDα finger cavity** — NOT FOUND |

P2Rank missed the finger cavity entirely. It's not deep enough or hydrophobic enough for P2Rank's training data. But P2Rank **did** find the palm groove — it just ranked it low (#3) because the NBD fuel pocket dominates the score.

---

### What this means for our docking

Our docking history, recast:

| Phase | Receptor | Site | What it actually tested |
|-------|----------|------|------------------------|
| June 1–2 | `hsc70-sbd-only.pdb` (531–615) | **SBDα fingers** (tri-helix) | Pocket 2 — the secondary, promiscuous finger cavity |
| June 3+ | PDB 4PO2 chain A | **SBDβ palm groove** | Pocket 1 — the primary binding site |

Our early lid-only runs weren't "wrong" — they captured a real pocket. But it's the secondary one, and binding there is flexible/non-specific. Our current 4PO2 approach targets the primary palm groove, which v2 says is where the peptide goes on the full-length protein — but only in the closed state with the lid present.

The key gap: 4PO2 has the palm but not the full fingers (it's the SBD domain, not full-length). So we have the right pocket but may be missing the lid's stabilizing effect.

ASSISTANT: Water turns out to be a major piece of the puzzle. They found that water molecules form a **transient hydrogen-bond network** that bridges three components together:

```
                    WATER BRIDGES
                    ╭──────────╮
    peptide ←······→  H₂O  ←······→ lid domain
      │                              (SBDα)
      │                              ~1–7 waters
      │                               
      └······→  H₂O  ←······→ SBDβ palm groove
              ~1–13 waters
```

Key findings:

- **Peptide ↔ lid**: ~1–7 water molecules bridge hydrophilic residues on the peptide to hydrophilic residues on the lid. These are dynamic — different waters connect at different times, different substrates use different patterns.

- **Extended chains**: Not just single waters. Chains of 2–4 water molecules relay interactions. For example, Abeta's E766 → 2 waters → K539 on the lid.

- **Peptide ↔ SBDβ**: ~1–13 water molecules bridge the peptide to the palm groove.

- **Pattern is substrate-specific and transient** — no single water bridge is conserved across all substrates.

### Why this matters

This explains the central mystery: **why removing the lid makes the peptide fall out of the SBDβ groove.** The lid isn't just physically capping the peptide — it's part of a water-mediated network. Without the lid, these water bridges collapse, the peptide loses its hydration shell coordination, and it drifts away. The paper speculates these transient waters "dynamically stabilize the closed conformation."

### Implication for docking

This is a limitation of HADDOCK3 — it uses implicit solvent (no explicit waters). We can't model these water bridges. If water-mediated lid-peptide contacts are critical, our docking may underestimate binding by missing this contribution.
